# Visual Translation Protocol

## Purpose and Scope

The Visual Translation Protocol is an optional reasoning protocol within the root [Creative Workflow](../CREATIVE_WORKFLOW.md). It converts communication intent, abstract language, brand intent, cultural direction, emotional tone and verbal concepts into observable visual principles and executable design decisions.

It is used when the intended meaning is sufficiently clear to discuss but not yet concrete enough to direct visual execution. It narrows ambiguity before detailed creative direction, world and scene reasoning.

This protocol is not:

- a complete generation prompt;
- a style preset;
- a mood-board generator;
- a fixed design formula;
- a collection of prompt keywords;
- an automatic replacement for creative judgement.

It does not create a YCOS mode, engine, Bootstrap sequence or independent workflow. It does not replace Project Understanding, Creative Intent, World Definition, Physical Logic, Material Behaviour, Lighting, Camera and Perspective, Composition, Scene Locks, Asset Blueprint, root Validation or Commercial Evaluation.

## Activation Conditions

Activate Visual Translation only when a project contains an important gap between what the work should communicate and what must become visually observable. Common activation conditions include:

- abstract or comparative direction, such as “more premium, but not luxurious”;
- cultural direction that requires contextual interpretation rather than literal symbols;
- emotional qualities that could produce several materially different visual treatments;
- a request to increase impact, clarity, youthfulness, restraint, trust or other communication qualities;
- a verbal product message that must become an icon, key visual, hierarchy or visual behaviour;
- conflicting adjectives, exclusions or boundaries that require a deliberate visual resolution;
- revision feedback that describes a desired feeling but not the observable change.

Do not activate it merely because a creative task exists. Skip it when the visual direction is already explicit, the task is purely technical, or translation would add no meaningful decision value.

Activation is task-specific and may be brief. It ends when a Selected Direction provides enough visual principles and decisions for the existing Creative Workflow to continue.

## Relationship with the Creative Workflow

The root Creative Workflow remains authoritative. Visual Translation operates inside its iterative reasoning and does not redefine its stages.

A typical integration is:

1. Project Understanding establishes the objective, brand, audience, platform, deliverable, constraints and required content.
2. Creative Intent identifies what the audience should notice, understand, feel or do.
3. Optional Visual Translation converts the unresolved communication intent into a Selected Direction expressed through observable visual decisions.
4. The remaining Creative Workflow establishes the world, physical and material logic, lighting, camera, composition, commercial evaluation, decision and execution strategy.
5. Root [Validation](../VALIDATION.md) remains mandatory.

Because creative reasoning is iterative, later world, material, lighting, camera, composition or commercial findings may require the translation to be refined. Visual Translation may inform those decisions but cannot overrule their established logic. A translated principle such as “lighter” does not authorize implausible lighting; “dynamic” does not authorize inconsistent perspective; “premium” does not justify reduced product or message clarity.

The protocol outputs decisions for the Creative Workflow. It does not output an independent execution workflow.

## Relationship with Asset Blueprint

Visual Translation and the optional [Asset Blueprint Protocol](../asset-blueprint/ASSET_BLUEPRINT_PROTOCOL.md) have separate responsibilities:

- **Visual Translation** converts communication intent and abstract language into contextual visual language and proposed execution decisions.
- **Asset Blueprint** preserves approved reusable asset identity and continuity across images, angles, scenes, styles and shots.

Visual Translation does not extract, approve, version or lock reusable asset identity. It does not create Reference Roles or duplicate Reference Separation. When reference separation is required for reusable asset continuity, use the Asset Blueprint workflow.

An active Blueprint may constrain translation. A proposed colour, material, silhouette or recognition change must be checked against Permanent Asset Locks. Visual Translation may propose a scene treatment, Controlled Variation, Temporary State Change or redesign, but the Asset Blueprint Protocol determines its asset change classification and approval requirements.

Translation quality cannot compensate for asset drift, and asset continuity cannot compensate for weak communication.

## Visual Translation Intake

Visual Translation Intake records only the information needed to resolve the communication-to-visual gap. It should inherit confirmed Project Understanding and Creative Intent rather than duplicate them.

Record, when applicable:

- source statement or direction;
- communication objective;
- intended audience and context;
- brand position and existing visual identity;
- deliverable, platform, viewing conditions and format;
- required content and hierarchy;
- desired audience response;
- explicit exclusions and “not this” boundaries;
- existing Scene Locks;
- active Asset Blueprint level, Asset Version and applicable Permanent Asset Locks;
- variable elements and permitted degree of change;
- cultural, regional, historical or category context;
- ambiguity, conflict and information requiring confirmation.

Do not convert an adjective directly into a visual formula during intake. First establish what the term means for this project, audience and communication objective.

## Communication Intent Extraction

Extract communication intent as an audience-facing outcome rather than a list of visual adjectives.

Determine:

- **Notice:** What should attract attention first?
- **Understand:** What message, benefit or distinction should become clear?
- **Feel:** What emotional response should support that understanding?
- **Do:** What action or next thought should the work encourage?
- **Remember:** What single idea, asset or recognition anchor should persist?
- **Avoid:** What incorrect reading, category signal or emotional response must not occur?

When several intentions compete, rank them. The primary communication intent governs the translation; secondary intentions may support it but should not weaken hierarchy or commercial readability.

## Semantic Decomposition

Decompose each abstract statement before translating it. Identify:

1. **Core meaning** — the intended quality in this project.
2. **Contrast condition** — what “more,” “less,” “stronger” or “without” is relative to.
3. **Positive signals** — behaviours that could express the quality.
4. **Excluded signals** — familiar treatments that would create the wrong reading.
5. **Context dependency** — how audience, market, medium, category and brand affect the meaning.
6. **Observable evidence** — what a reviewer could actually see and assess.
7. **Trade-offs** — what may be lost if the quality is pushed too far.

For example, “youthful without looking childish” does not prescribe bright primary colours or playful illustrations. It may mean faster hierarchy, fresher contrast, more direct language and energetic rhythm while retaining disciplined typography, controlled detail and category credibility. The correct translation depends on the project context.

## Cultural and Contextual Interpretation

Cultural language must be treated as contextual direction, not as a fixed visual vocabulary.

Before proposing decisions:

- identify the relevant place, audience, period, subculture, category and communication setting;
- distinguish contemporary lived culture from historical, ceremonial, touristic or exported imagery;
- determine whether the request concerns values, restraint, rhythm, craft, behaviour, spatial organisation, material sensibility, graphic conventions or explicit symbols;
- identify signals that may be stereotypical, sacred, politically sensitive, overused or inappropriate for the brand;
- distinguish recognition needed by the intended audience from decoration added for outsiders;
- calibrate how explicit or implicit the cultural signal should be.

“More Japanese, but not traditionally Japanese,” for example, requires clarification of audience, market, brand and relevant contemporary context. It must not automatically produce historic motifs, national symbols, calligraphic effects or a predetermined palette.

When cultural interpretation cannot be made responsibly from available information, keep the uncertainty visible and request targeted clarification before execution.

## Visual Language Dimensions

Translate intent across only the dimensions that materially support the project. Each selected dimension should state the proposed behaviour, its communication purpose and relevant limits.

### Shape Language

Consider geometry, curvature, edge character, proportion, silhouette, repetition and directional energy. Shape decisions must remain compatible with physical logic and Permanent Asset Locks.

### Colour Behaviour

Consider contrast, saturation relationships, temperature, value structure, accent allocation, colour proportion and interaction with brand colours. Define behaviour rather than treating a named colour as a complete direction.

### Typography Character

Consider scale contrast, weight, width, spacing, case, rhythm, alignment and reading pace. Typography must preserve legibility, language requirements and brand authority.

### Material Expression

Consider whether materials should communicate precision, warmth, utility, craft, softness, durability, novelty or restraint through credible surface behaviour. The root workflow remains authoritative for material realism.

### Lighting Behaviour

Consider contrast, softness, direction, falloff, exposure priority and atmospheric role. Lighting must emerge from and remain consistent with the established world.

### Composition and Hierarchy

Consider dominance, reading order, scale relationships, density, negative space, cropping, rhythm and supporting emphasis. The Hero Subject and communication objective remain primary.

### Graphic Vocabulary

Consider line, frame, field, pattern, icon, diagram, texture, mark and image-treatment behaviours. Vocabulary should form a coherent system rather than an assortment of decorative devices.

### Decorative Density

Define the amount, distribution and purpose of nonessential detail. Greater impact may come from stronger hierarchy, scale or contrast rather than additional elements.

### Motion Character, When Applicable

Consider pace, continuity, acceleration, easing character, transition logic, repetition and stillness. Motion should support comprehension and emotional tone rather than distract from the message.

### Emotional Tone

Define the intended emotional reading and the visual evidence that should create it. Avoid treating emotion as a single colour or effect.

### Commercial Readability

Consider immediate subject recognition, message clarity, brand recognition, platform legibility, product desirability and suitability at the expected viewing size and duration.

### Cultural Signal Strength

Define whether cultural signals should be absent, implicit, legible or explicit. State which signals are appropriate and which are excluded; do not assume stronger signalling is better.

### Traditional versus Contemporary Balance

Define how historical continuity and present-day relevance should relate. This balance may be expressed through principles, craft, structure or behaviour without literal traditional motifs.

## Cliché and Stereotype Avoidance

For every culturally or emotionally loaded direction:

1. List the first obvious visual associations.
2. Identify which are contextually justified, overused, reductive or excluded.
3. Look for deeper structural expressions of the intent, such as hierarchy, rhythm, proportion, restraint, material behaviour or interaction.
4. Test whether the direction respects the intended audience rather than performing identity for an external gaze.
5. Remove signals that do not carry a clear communication function.

Avoidance does not mean removing all recognisable signals. It means selecting them deliberately, at an appropriate strength, with contextual justification.

## From Abstract Statements to Observable Decisions

Translate each important statement through the following chain:

**Source statement → contextual meaning → communication job → visual principles → observable decisions → exclusions → validation evidence**

Examples:

| Source statement | Context-dependent interpretation | Observable decisions | Exclusions / limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| More premium, but not luxurious | Increase confidence, care and perceived quality while remaining accessible | Fewer competing accents; disciplined spacing; precise hierarchy; credible material detail; controlled contrast | No automatic use of gold, black, marble, excessive gloss or status symbolism |
| Youthful without looking childish | Communicate current energy and openness without reducing credibility | Quicker reading rhythm; fresh accent contrast; direct graphic forms; controlled asymmetry; concise hierarchy | No automatic primary-colour palette, toy-like shapes or juvenile illustration |
| Stronger impact without adding clutter | Improve the speed and force of the primary read | Increase hero scale or contrast; reduce secondary competition; simplify grouping; strengthen focal isolation | Do not add effects, props, text or decoration solely to create intensity |
| Turn the product message into an icon | Make one benefit recognisable at small size | Isolate the benefit's action or relationship; reduce it to one distinctive silhouette or interaction; test at intended size | Do not encode multiple claims or rely on unexplained symbolism |

These examples demonstrate reasoning patterns, not reusable formulas.

## Selected Direction and Decision Rules

Visual Translation should converge on one Selected Direction when the evidence supports a clear choice.

Select the direction that best:

- serves the primary communication intent;
- fits the brand, audience, deliverable and context;
- remains commercially readable;
- respects Scene Locks and Permanent Asset Locks;
- can be executed coherently through the authoritative Creative Workflow;
- avoids unjustified cultural shortcuts and decorative excess;
- provides observable evidence for validation.

Alternative directions should remain only when they offer materially different and useful strategies or when explicitly requested. Do not present several equal adjective bundles in place of a decision.

When objectives conflict, preserve the primary intent and document the trade-off. When uncertainty would materially change the outcome, request clarification rather than resolving it through a cliché or silent assumption.

## Scene Locks and Asset Locks Interaction

Visual Translation may propose changes but does not silently alter locks.

- A proposal affecting environment, camera, lighting, composition, perspective, hero placement or global colour mood must be reconciled with existing Scene Locks under the Creative Workflow.
- A proposal affecting intrinsic silhouette, proportions, structure, markings, materials, primary colour logic, logos or recognition anchors must be reconciled with Permanent Asset Locks under the Asset Blueprint Protocol.
- A local translation request must preserve unrelated locks.
- If a visual decision conflicts with a lock, record the conflict and revise the translation, deliberately revise the Scene Lock, or use the Asset Blueprint change classification as applicable.

A translated direction is not authority to weaken, reinterpret or overwrite a lock.

## Variable Assets and Controlled Variations Interaction

Variable Assets belong to the current project world. Controlled Variations belong to an approved reusable asset version. They are not interchangeable.

Visual Translation may specify how a Variable Asset should support communication without redefining unrelated Scene Locks. When an active Blueprint exists, any recurring change to asset identity must be checked against approved Controlled Variations and Permitted Variation.

Classify asset-related proposals through the Asset Blueprint Protocol as one of:

- Permanent Asset Lock;
- Controlled Variation;
- Temporary State Change;
- New Version / Redesign.

Visual Translation can recommend the communication value of a variation, but it cannot approve or activate the variation. Necessary physical, lighting, material and interaction consequences remain governed by the Creative Workflow.

## Translation Output Format

Use the smallest output that gives the next Creative Workflow stage clear direction. A complete output may contain:

1. **Activation reason** — the ambiguity or communication-to-visual gap being resolved.
2. **Communication Intent** — ranked Notice, Understand, Feel, Do, Remember and Avoid outcomes.
3. **Context interpretation** — audience, brand, market, cultural and medium-specific meaning.
4. **Semantic decomposition** — core meanings, positive signals, exclusions and trade-offs.
5. **Selected Direction** — one concise directional statement and its rationale.
6. **Visual language decisions** — only the applicable dimensions, each with observable decisions and limits.
7. **Lock interaction** — Scene Locks, Permanent Asset Locks and unresolved conflicts affected by the direction.
8. **Variation classification** — relevant Variable Assets, Controlled Variations or Temporary State Changes.
9. **Execution handoff** — decisions the Creative Workflow must resolve through world, physical, material, lighting, camera, composition and commercial reasoning.
10. **Validation evidence** — observable criteria for determining whether the translation succeeded.
11. **Uncertainties** — assumptions or questions that could materially change the direction.

The output is a reasoning and decision record, not a generation prompt.

## Known Failure Modes

- Treating an adjective, culture or audience as a fixed visual formula.
- Substituting prompt keywords for contextual interpretation.
- Producing a mood-board description without making decisions.
- Adding decoration instead of improving hierarchy or communication.
- Translating every dimension when only a few are relevant.
- Optimising emotional tone while weakening product, message or brand clarity.
- Using literal cultural motifs without contextual justification.
- Confusing contemporary cultural expression with historical or touristic imagery.
- Presenting multiple undifferentiated directions instead of selecting one.
- Treating translated material or lighting language as permission to violate world logic.
- Silently changing Scene Locks or Permanent Asset Locks.
- Confusing Variable Assets, Controlled Variations and Temporary State Changes.
- Treating a proposed direction as approved reusable asset identity.
- Generating a complete execution prompt instead of handing decisions back to the Creative Workflow.
- Evaluating success by aesthetic appeal alone.

## Validation Criteria

Visual Translation passes only when:

- activation was necessary and remained optional;
- the output traces back to confirmed Project Understanding and Creative Intent;
- abstract language has a context-dependent meaning rather than a fixed formula;
- the primary communication objective and audience response are clear;
- selected visual dimensions contain observable, executable decisions;
- the Selected Direction is decisive and commercially readable;
- exclusions and trade-offs prevent the most likely incorrect readings;
- cultural signals are contextual, proportionate and free from unjustified stereotype;
- the protocol has not created a new mode, engine, Bootstrap sequence or parallel workflow;
- world, physical, material, lighting, camera, composition and commercial authority remains with the Creative Workflow;
- Scene Locks remain authoritative unless deliberately revised;
- Permanent Asset Locks and asset change classifications remain authoritative under Asset Blueprint;
- Reference Separation has not been duplicated outside Asset Blueprint;
- the output is model-neutral, tool-neutral and not prompt-centric;
- the decisions provide evidence that can be assessed during mandatory root Validation.

After translation-specific criteria pass, the project must still complete every applicable Creative Workflow stage and the root Validation. When Asset Blueprint Level 1 or Level 2 is active, its required execution and asset validation extensions also apply.
